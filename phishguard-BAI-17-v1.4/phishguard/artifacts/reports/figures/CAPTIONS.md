# Figures for the report

Generated by `phishguard figures` from `artifacts/reports/evaluation.json`. Every number below is read from that file, so re-running the evaluation and this command keeps figures and captions in step.

Insert the `.png` files into Word (they are 220 dpi at 6.5 in wide); use the `.svg` files for LaTeX or anywhere that scales.

## Figure 1 — Outcomes on the PG-HARD benchmark

File: `fig01_pg_hard_outcomes.png`

**Figure 1.** Outcomes on PG-HARD, 192 records built from 24 hand-designed cases that each defeat a named detection cue. All three systems were first tuned to the same false-alarm budget on ordinary mail. PhishGuard makes 8 wrong automatic decisions, all in case HN-REBRAND (silent delivery 0.0%, false block 8.3%); the text-only filter blocks 51% of the hard legitimate mail and the rule checklist delivers 77% of the hard phishing unwarned. PhishGuard's result is bought with escalation: it sends 58% of these hard records, and 11.0% of ordinary mail, to a human. Retrained with 2 further seeds, PhishGuard delivered 0–1 hard phishing records unwarned and blocked 7–8 hard legitimate ones, so the figure shows one draw from that range.

## Figure 2 — Robustness against query budget

File: `fig02_attack_budget.png`

**Figure 2.** Left: share of 48 hard phishing records delivered with no warning after a budgeted black-box attack, at increasing query budgets. The text-only filter rises from 0% to 90% at 120 queries; PhishGuard delivers 0% against a score-minimising attacker; an adaptive attacker that targets the abstention control reaches 6% (3 of 48) at 40 queries and 6% at 120. Right: the attack is not without effect - the share PhishGuard auto-blocks falls from 52% to 6% as the attacker spends queries, and at 120 queries 88% are escalated to review. Every attacker is graded by the deployed decision, not by the score it optimised.

## Figure 3 — Which ensemble members the attack could move

File: `fig03_member_movement.png`

**Figure 3.** Mean score of each ensemble member on the hard phishing records before and after the strongest adaptive attack (120 queries): rules 0.48 to 0.14; engineered 0.69 to 0.38; wordtfidf 0.85 to 0.78; charngram 0.82 to 0.76. The attack moves rules most and charngram least. Because escalation needs only one reader to keep objecting, the members' weaknesses matter only where they line up, which is the mechanism behind Figure 2.

## Figure 4 — Robustness by attack family

File: `fig04_attack_families.png`

**Figure 4.** Hard phishing delivered with no warning when the attacker is restricted to one attack family at a time (budget 40). The text-only filter is most exposed to A-UNI (unicode and homoglyph obfuscation, 15%); against PhishGuard the worst family is A-HDR (2%).

## Figure 5 — ROC curve on a logarithmic false-positive axis

File: `fig05_roc_log_fpr.png`

**Figure 5.** Receiver operating characteristic on the ordinary held-out test set, with the false-positive rate on a logarithmic axis so that the low-FPR region a mail gateway actually operates in is visible. The marker is the 0.5 cut-off used for the clean metrics (FPR 0.10%, recall 97.56%); the deployed policy has two thresholds and an abstention band rather than one cut-off. ROC AUC: PhishGuard 0.9983, the text-only filter 0.9986 and the rule checklist 0.9446. Ranking on ordinary mail already separates the systems, and Figures 1 and 2 show the gap widening on hard and attacked mail.

## Figure 6 — Calibration

File: `fig06_calibration.png`

**Figure 6.** Reliability of the calibrated score (isotonic; ECE 0.0077, uncalibrated 0.0093). 98% of test messages score below 0.025 or above 0.975, as the histogram shows, so the low ECE reflects strong separation on this corpus rather than a hard calibration problem solved. Hollow markers are bins holding fewer than ten messages, which carry no reliable estimate.

## Figure 7 — Feature-family ablation

File: `fig07_modality_ablation.png`

**Figure 7.** Errors per 1,000 messages (missed phishing plus false alarms) for the engineered member trained on each combination of the three feature families. The weakest single family (behavioral) makes 65.0 errors per 1,000, 4.7 times the best pair; all three together make the fewest (13.4), 0.5 fewer than the next best.

## Figure 8 — Operating point: cost and robustness

File: `fig08_operating_point.png`

**Figure 8.** Left: expected cost of handling benign traffic, per 1,000 messages, at 2% phishing prevalence with 10% hard traffic and illustrative costs in analyst reviews (missed phish 200, false block 10), for the deployed default (262; 121 per 1,000 to a human), the minimax-regret policy (191; 116 per 1,000 to a human) and the cost-optimal policy (191; 116 per 1,000 to a human). Right: the share of hard phishing each policy delivers with no warning under the adaptive attack: the deployed default 6% (3 of 48), the minimax-regret policy 12% (6 of 48) and the cost-optimal policy 12% (6 of 48). The cost model prices ordinary mail, but an attacker adapts to the deployed policy; that is why the more conservative default is kept and its benign-traffic price is reported.

## Figure 9 — Drift monitoring

File: `fig09_drift.png`

**Figure 9.** Worst population stability index (PSI) across the monitored quantities for 10 simulated weeks of 400 messages. From week 6 a quarter of traffic is mail unlike the training data. 0 of 5 stable weeks and 5 of 5 shifted weeks are flagged (moderate or significant); 1 of the shifted weeks reach the significant level. The thresholds (moderate 0.36, significant 0.53) are the 95th and 99th percentiles of the worst-quantity PSI of stable, campaign-structured windows, not the textbook 0.10 and 0.25, which assume independent samples and would fire on most stable weeks of campaign-structured mail.
